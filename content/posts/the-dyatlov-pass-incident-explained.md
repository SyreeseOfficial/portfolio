## What happened

In February 1959, ten hikers set out across the northern Ural Mountains in the Soviet Union. One turned back early with an illness. The other nine never came home.

When searchers reached their camp, the tent was still standing. It had been slashed open from the inside. Someone had cut their way out in the dark, in deep snow, at around -30°C.

Their boots were still inside. The footprints leading away were made by bare feet and socks. They ran downhill toward the tree line, about a mile off, in a straight line, as if everyone had been running from the same thing.

The first bodies lay at the edge of the forest, in their underwear, beside the remains of a small fire. More were found between the tent and the trees, as if they had tried to turn back.

The last four were found two months later, buried in a ravine under snow. Those were the ones with the injuries.

## Scary facts

- The tent was cut open from the inside, not torn from the outside.
- Nine experienced hikers left their boots and coats behind in subzero weather.
- Searchers found no tracks from anyone else near the camp.
- Three of the hikers had crushed ribs or a fractured skull. A doctor compared the force to a car crash, yet their skin showed no bruising or cuts.
- One woman was missing her tongue and her eyes.
- Some of their clothing carried traces of radiation.
- Searchers reported that some of the bodies had an orange tint to the skin.
- The Mansi people, who live in the region, call the nearby peak Kholat Syakhl, often translated as "Dead Mountain."
- The case files stayed classified in the Soviet Union for decades.

## The official answer

The investigation ended with one sentence: the hikers died because of "a compelling natural force." Nobody has said what that force was.

Whatever made nine experienced hikers cut open their own tent and run barefoot into the dark was worse than the cold they were running into.
